# Earth development limits

## Summary

The `earth-development-limits` branch imagines coordinated global restrictions on Earth's environmental burdens while permitting regulated off-world development. Persistent paying demand could turn those restrictions into an incentive for a new commercial and state-supported space race, even while Earth remains broadly livable.

## Reasoning

### Scope and branch: `earth-development-limits`

- **Assumptions:** Major governments maintain credible, coordinated limits that cannot readily be avoided by moving production to another country. Customers still pay enough for some additional output; regulated off-world activity is permitted; terrestrial industry remains capable of building the initial equipment.
- **Divergence:** Earth still has resources and productive capacity, but society restricts some additional environmental costs. This is distinct from the worsening conditions in [[wiki/concepts/climate-adaptation-pressure]] and does not inherit that branch or presume exhausted resources.
- **Consequences:** Firms first pursue efficiency and existing capacity. Where legal terrestrial alternatives cannot meet profitable demand, some invest off-world. States may support independent access to supply rather than leave their industries dependent on foreign providers.
- **Dependencies:** Shared physical laws, effective international implementation, a viable transition supply chain, paying demand, and an off-world service that beats available alternatives on sufficient customer-relevant criteria. The computing route additionally depends on [[wiki/tech/orbital-data-centers]]; the full mechanism does not depend on the existing orbital accelerator. AI-led demand and state support remain optional mechanisms described in [[wiki/concepts/ai-driven-industrial-demand]] and [[wiki/society/state-funded-space-industry]], not automatically inherited branches.
- **Grounding:** `speculative` for the complete transition to expanding off-world industry. Limits and allocation systems have present-day precedents; economical orbital industry at the proposed scale is unvalidated. This engineering grade is not a prediction that a global agreement will be adopted.

### Restrictions and effective demand

The institutional design is described in [[wiki/society/global-environmental-governance]]: coordinated coverage and enforcement, distinct environmental accounts, transition allocations, and regulation of off-world activity. Those arrangements remain assumptions or open choices within this branch.

A cap on electricity or environmental burden can still allow more output through efficiency: with the same electricity allocation and twice the useful computation per unit energy, delivered computation can double. These are illustrative ratios. Off-world pressure persists only where effective demand outgrows such improvements and other legal supply options.

### Corporate competition and a new space race

Companies compare scarce existing terrestrial capacity, efficiency upgrades, changed products, abandoned orders, and off-world supply. Space need not beat a historical unrestricted terrestrial price, but must deliver a service customers will actually buy after financing, failures, maintenance, communications, and replacement costs. Higher prices can reduce demand.

In the computing route, Earth customers buy AI applications or other digital services while energy collection, computation, and much of heat rejection occur off-world. The proposed progression is paying customers, repeat construction orders, expanded transport and maintenance, and only then selected local materials and processing where economical. It does not assume that complete semiconductor fabrication relocates immediately or that bulk exports to Earth are profitable.

First-mover advantage comes from operating capacity, surveyed and usable resource sources, infrastructure, experience, permissions, long-term contracts, and integration with customers. A claim or early arrival alone does not establish ownership, production, or market control. Learning and greater utilization could lower costs, which could attract more customers and finance further construction. That feedback is conditional: cheap computation does not guarantee the best model or application, and customers also care about service quality, latency, reliability, and data requirements.

Low production cost and low selling price are separate. A firm may discount to gain customers or retain higher margins when alternatives are scarce. Durable dominance needs additional barriers, such as difficult switching or hard-to-reproduce infrastructure; being first does not guarantee a monopoly.

Governments might procure capacity, share infrastructure, finance research, or support competing supply to protect industrial autonomy. This can coexist with cooperation on global environmental limits: states agree on Earth's constraints while competing over the new supply. Such procurement is a possible link to `state-funded-expansion`, not an assumption that all state-funded projects adopt this agreement.

## Validation

### Energy, emissions, and solar albedo

More AI electricity demand does not establish an equal growth rate in global emissions. The result depends on the marginal energy supply, efficiency, and changes elsewhere in the economy. IEA projects rising data-centre demand and emissions but does not establish the proposed AI-driven global emissions surge. A surge remains an additional scenario assumption, not a shared premise. [IEA: Key Questions on Energy and AI](https://www.iea.org/reports/key-questions-on-energy-and-ai/executive-summary)

Solar installations can change surface albedo, vegetation, and local energy exchanges. A study of worldwide solar-panel expansion found smaller albedo reductions than some simplified modelling assumptions. [Communications Earth & Environment: solar expansion and albedo](https://www.nature.com/articles/s43247-024-01619-w) IPCC assesses albedo effects as small compared with the mitigation benefits in the deployment contexts it evaluates. This does not validate a blanket solar ban or exempt all sites from environmental assessment. [IPCC AR6 WGIII, Chapter 6](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/wg3/chapter/chapter-6/)

For scale only, globally averaged incoming sunlight is about **340 W/m²**. If whole-planet albedo hypothetically falls by **0.001**, the initial additional absorption is **340 × 0.001 = 0.34 W/m²**. This is a hypothetical planetary change, including atmospheric effects, not an estimate of current or future photovoltaic deployment and not a temperature prediction. Actual effects require deployment area, location, cloud cover, original surface, vegetation, and displaced emissions. [NASA: Earth's energy budget](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/climate-and-earths-energy-budget/)

Sunlight converted to electricity and later heat is the same energy, not two additions. The comparison must include what would otherwise have been absorbed or reflected. Off-world infrastructure still obeys conservation and must reject heat; its power and radiator checks are in [[wiki/tech/orbital-data-centers]]. Environmental restrictions change incentives, not physical costs.

### Institutional evidence

The allocation precedents and legal limits supporting the institutional assumptions are recorded in [[wiki/society/global-environmental-governance]]. They do not establish global agreement or the profitability of moving production off-world.

### Consistency and motive

Checked [[canon/laws]], [[canon/timeline]], and existing transport drafts for [[sessions/2026-11-19-drivers-of-space-expansion]]. No canon agreement, deployment date, resource claim, or monopoly is introduced. [[wiki/tech/orbital-transportation-network]] remains an independently speculative candidate, not required infrastructure or evidence of demand.

Customers pay for usable services, investors fund anticipated returns, and states may pay for supply access and resilience. Their motives differ. Confidence in lasting restrictions matters: policy reversal, falling demand, cheaper terrestrial solutions, or disappointing technical performance can strand investments.

## Consequences

- Allocation and access can affect who benefits before space industry succeeds; the distributional implications are described in [[wiki/society/global-environmental-governance]].
- Costly infrastructure and difficult customer switching could produce concentrated suppliers; open interfaces and shared services could instead support competition. Overestimated demand could produce overcapacity and exits. These are conditional outcomes, not a forecast of inevitable monopoly.
- A policy protecting Earth can coexist with intense state and corporate rivalry over off-world supply. Government support can speed development or sustain uneconomic projects.
- Automated facilities may grow much faster than resident populations. Permanent settlement still needs separate economic and social justification in [[wiki/concepts/space-expansion-motives]].
- `earth-centered-development`, defined in [[wiki/concepts/space-expansion-motives]], remains a viable alternative if terrestrial improvements and restrained demand avoid large-scale off-world expansion.

## Open questions

- The branch's unresolved institutional design and lifecycle-accounting checks are recorded in [[wiki/society/global-environmental-governance]].
- Which paying customers and services support off-world investment after efficiency gains, higher prices, and terrestrial alternatives are accounted for?
- What operating costs, utilization, financing, and technical performance create a sustainable advantage rather than stranded investment?
- Which switching costs, infrastructure access rules, and state procurement arrangements determine concentration, competition, and distribution of gains?

These unresolved checks are tracked in [[open-questions]].
